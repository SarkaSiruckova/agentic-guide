Once you know which jobs suit AI, the next question is which assistant to use for them. This page names the main options without ranking them, and sets out the questions that decide it for you.

**In one line:** the major assistants are close enough in everyday ability that the best choice usually depends on where your work already lives, the data terms you need, and what you mostly want it for.

## The jargon: concepts covered on this page

- **AI assistant:** a chat app built on a language model, such as ChatGPT or Claude
- **Business plan:** a paid plan for organisations, usually with stricter data terms
- **Open-weight model:** a model whose files can be downloaded and run outside the maker's own service

## Why it matters

Picking an assistant feels like a big decision, and the headlines make it sound like one model is always ahead. In practice the lead changes often, and for drafting, summarising and explaining the main assistants all do well.

<mark>The assistant that fits your tools and your data rules will serve you better than the one topping this month's leaderboard.</mark>

## How it works

**The main general assistants, as of October 2026.** Each is a chat app built on its maker's own models (see [what an LLM is](/start/what-an-llm-is/)), in alphabetical order:

- **ChatGPT**, from OpenAI
- **Claude**, from Anthropic
- **Gemini**, from Google, also built into Google Workspace
- **Grok**, from xAI, also available inside the X social network
- **Microsoft Copilot**, also built into Microsoft 365 for work accounts; Microsoft has announced it is merging its personal and work Copilot apps into one
- **Vibe**, from Mistral AI, a French company; this assistant was called Le Chat until 2026
- **Open-weight options**, models you can download and run yourself or through another service, such as Meta's Llama, Mistral's open models, DeepSeek and Qwen

Most offer a free tier, paid personal plans and business plans. Names and plans change often, so check each maker's own site.

**The questions that decide it.**

1. **Where does your work already live?** If your team runs on Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, the assistant built into it can see your files with less setup.
2. **What data terms do you need?** Free and personal plans often allow your chats to be used for training unless you switch it off. Business plans and APIs usually do not. Check the terms for the exact plan, not just the company.
3. **What do you mostly need?** Writing, coding, research with web search, images, voice, or keeping data on your own machines. Each assistant has different strengths and features here, and they shift with each release.

## In practice

Try two or three on the same real task from your own work, not a puzzle from the internet. Compare the results, how easily each fits your tools, and what the plan's data terms say. Many people end up using one main assistant and a second for particular jobs.

## Worked example

Sam at Bramley's, the two-person bakery, already uses Google Workspace for email and the order spreadsheet, so tries Gemini there first. Jo, the freelance researcher, compares two assistants on summarising a report and keeps the one whose answers are easier to check against sources. Sample Ventures, the fictional fund, picks a business plan whose terms say client data is not used for training, after reading [data terms at a glance](/models/data-terms-at-a-glance/).

## Costs and limits

All the main assistants have a free tier with tighter limits, and paid plans for heavier use. Plan types and what they cost are covered on the next page.

Every assistant still makes confident mistakes. Switching provider does not remove the need to [check the output](/start/hallucination-and-grounding/).

**Why this guide uses Claude.** The practical, step-by-step pages use Claude, made by Anthropic, as their example. It is one reasonable choice, and a guide needs one set of menus to point at. The concepts, such as tools, agents and connecting your data, work the same way across the major assistants.
